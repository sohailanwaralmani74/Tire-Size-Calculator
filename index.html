Worked Example: 265/70R17 vs. 285/75R17

Switching from a 265/70R17 to a 285/75R17 increases overall tire diameter by 56.5 mm (+2.22 in or +7.04%), adds 20 mm (+0.79 in) of section width, and raises theoretical ground clearance at the axle by 28.25 mm (about 28 mm / 1.11 in). When your speedometer reads 60 mph, your approximate actual road speed is 64.2 mph.

Tire & Wheel Geometry Reference Guide

Written for vehicle owners comparing tire sizes, wheel offsets, and axle ratios. Every section separates what can be calculated from sidewall numbers from what must be checked physically on the vehicle.

1. What does 265/70R17 actually mean?

A 265/70R17 tire has a nominal section width of 265 millimeters, a sidewall height equal to 70% of that width (185.5 mm), radial cord construction (R), and seats on a 17-inch (431.8 mm) wheel rim, giving an overall diameter of 802.8 mm (31.61 inches).

The mistake people make most often is treating the middle number—70—as a fixed measurement in millimeters. It is a percentage of the width. At a constant 17-inch rim diameter, changing either the width or the aspect ratio changes your sidewall height and overall tire diameter:

  • 265/70R17: 265 mm × 0.70 = 185.5 mm sidewall → 431.8 + (2 × 185.5) = 802.8 mm (31.61") diameter.
  • 265/65R17 (lower aspect ratio, same width): 265 mm × 0.65 = 172.25 mm sidewall → 776.3 mm (30.56") diameter, losing 26.5 mm of height.
  • 285/70R17 (wider tire, same 70% aspect ratio): 285 mm × 0.70 = 199.5 mm sidewall → 830.8 mm (32.71") diameter, gaining 28 mm of height even though the aspect number stayed 70.

2. How to read a tire size (using 275/65R18)

In the metric code 275/65R18, 275 is the nominal cross-section width in millimeters from sidewall to sidewall, 65 is the sidewall aspect ratio in percent, R designates radial construction, and 18 is the wheel bead-seat diameter in inches.

Walking through the math for 275/65R18 step by step shows why metric tire codes mix millimeters and inches:

  1. Width: 275 mm ÷ 25.4 = 10.83 inches wide when mounted on its industry measuring rim width.
  2. One sidewall height: 65% of 275 mm = 275 × 0.65 = 178.75 mm (7.04 inches).
  3. Wheel rim diameter: 18 inches × 25.4 = 457.2 mm.
  4. Overall tire height (diameter): Because the wheel sits in the center with a sidewall above it and a sidewall below it, add the rim plus two sidewalls: 457.2 mm + (2 × 178.75 mm) = 814.7 mm (32.07 inches).

3. What changes when you install a larger tire?

Installing a larger tire changes two distinct categories: pure geometric values (diameter, axle ground clearance, rolling circumference, speedometer ratio, and effective gearing) that can be calculated from the sidewall numbers, and physical vehicle clearances (fender liners, upper control arms, body mounts, and steering lock) that depend entirely on your specific vehicle.

Using the jump from 265/70R17 to 285/75R17 as our benchmark, here is the strict separation between math and hardware:

What is pure math (Calculated)

  • Overall diameter: increases by +56.5 mm (+2.22 in).
  • Axle ground clearance: increases by half the diameter change (+28.25 mm / +1.11 in) because only the bottom radius sits between the hub and the road.
  • Arch gap reduction: the top radius also grows by 28.25 mm, moving the tread closer to the upper fender arch.
  • Section width: grows by +20 mm (+0.79 in) total (10 mm per side on an identical wheel offset).
  • Speedometer & odometer: 7.04% larger circumference means 42 fewer revolutions per mile (638 → 596 revs/mi).
  • Effective gearing: a 3.73 axle drops to an effective 3.48:1 ratio.

What depends on the vehicle (Physical)

  • Steering sweep arc: a tire that is 28.25 mm larger in radius and 20 mm wider swings through a much wider arc at full steering lock, often contacting front bumper valances or rear cab mounts first.
  • Upper control arm (UCA) clearance: on independent front suspension (IFS) trucks and SUVs, theextra 10 mm of inner sidewall width can contact the UCA ball-joint boss if wheel offset is too positive.
  • Suspension compression: static driveway clearance does not equal clearance when hitting a bump with the wheels turned.

4. What happens when you increase tire diameter?

Increasing tire diameter lengthens the rolling lever arm between your axle and the road: every 1% increase in diameter increases distance traveled per wheel revolution by 1%, raises actual road speed relative to indicated speedometer speed by 1%, and reduces mechanical axle torque multiplication by nearly 1%.

Consider a pickup or SUV that comes from the factory with 265/70R17 (802.8 mm diameter) and a 3.73:1 rear axle ratio. If you step up to 285/75R17 (859.3 mm diameter, a +7.04% increase):

  • Ground clearance vs. wheel-well room: You gain 28.25 mm (1.11 in) of clearance under the differential pumpkin, while simultaneously losing 28.25 mm of clearance at the top, front, and rear of the wheel opening.
  • Speedometer under-reading: Your vehicle’s speed sensor counts shaft revolutions and assumes each tire turn covers 2,522 mm. The new tire covers 2,700 mm. At an indicated 70 mph, you are actually traveling 70 × (859.3 ÷ 802.8) = 74.9 mph.
  • Gearing & braking leverage: The effective final drive ratio drops to 3.73 × (802.8 ÷ 859.3) = 3.48:1. Restoring the factory mechanical leverage would require re-gearing the differentials to 3.73 × (859.3 ÷ 802.8) = 3.99:1 (commonly a 4.10:1 gear set). Just as the engine loses mechanical advantage to turn the taller tire, the brake rotors also act against a 7% longer lever arm.

5. Tire size vs. wheel size: why a bigger wheel does not mean a bigger tire

A larger wheel diameter (rim size) does not automatically make the overall tire taller; in factory trim packages and plus-sizing, stepping up from a 17-inch to an 18-inch or 20-inch wheel is paired with a lower aspect ratio so the outer tire diameter stays virtually identical.

Automakers frequently offer 17-inch, 18-inch, and 20-inch wheels on the exact same truck or SUV without recalibrating the speedometer or changing suspension height. They do this by trading rubber sidewall height for aluminum wheel diameter:

6. How wheel offset changes fitment (with a numeric example)

Wheel offset (measured in millimeters and marked ET) is the distance from the wheel’s centerline to its hub mounting surface; decreasing offset pushes the wheel outward toward the fender and increases track width, while widening a wheel adds half of the extra width to both the inner and outer edges.

To see how wheel width and offset combine, compare a stock 17x8 ET45 wheel against an aftermarket 18x9 ET20 wheel:

Even though the new 9-inch wheel is 25.4 mm wider overall, dropping the offset by 25 mm (from +45 to +20) moves the inner rim edge 12.3 mm farther away from the suspension strut while pushing the outer rim lip 37.7 mm (1.48 in) farther outward toward the fender lip.

7. Why two tires with similar diameters can fit differently

Two tires with nearly identical calculated diameters can fit completely differently on the vehicle because section width, wheel rim width, wheel offset, tread shoulder profile, and manufacturer molding tolerances control where the outer corner of the tire swings during steering and suspension travel.

Compare a tall-and-narrow 255/80R17 (839.8 mm / 33.06 in diameter, 255 mm width) with a wider 285/70R17 (830.8 mm / 32.71 in diameter, 285 mm width). On paper, the 255/80R17 is 9 mm taller. Yet on many four-wheel-drive trucks, the 255/80R17 clears the upper control arm and cab mount on factory wheels while the shorter 285/70R17 rubs. Why?

  • Section and tread width: The 285 tire is 30 mm (1.18 inches) wider. Its outer shoulder sits 15 mm farther out on both sides, increasing its diagonal scrub radius when you turn the steering wheel.
  • Measuring rim width effect: Every metric tire specification is measured on a specific reference rim width. Mounting a tire on a wheel that is 1 inch wider than the measuring rim pulls the beads apart and increases actual section width by roughly 0.4 inches (10 mm) rule-of-thumb.
  • Actual vs. nominal manufacturer dimensions: Industry standards (ETRTO and TRA) allow tolerances of several percent around nominal sidewall codes, and mud-terrain or rugged-terrain tires with aggressive side lugs and square shoulders often measure wider at the shoulder block than highway all-season tires stamped with the exact same size code.

8. Tire pressure: what this tool will not tell you, and why

This calculator does not output recommended tire pressure (PSI or bar) because proper inflation pressure cannot be derived from tire dimensions alone; it is governed by your vehicle’s axle weight ratings, tire load construction (P-metric, Euro-metric, XL, or LT), and industry load-inflation tables.

If you are running your vehicle’s factory tire size and load specification, your cold inflation pressure is printed on the driver’s door-jamb placard and in the owner’s manual. Never inflate to the “Max Press” number molded into the tire sidewall unless your vehicle placard or load table specifically calls for it. Furthermore, switching from a standard passenger tire (such as a P265/70R17 113T rated at 35 PSI) to a heavy-duty Light Truck tire (such as an LT285/75R17 Load Range E) changes the internal casing construction—an LT tire typically requires higher cold inflation pressure than a P-metric tire to carry the exact same axle weight safely without overheating. Always consult the tire manufacturer’s published load-and-inflation tables and a qualified tire technician.

Methodology & Formulas

Wanjaaro computes all outputs from standard nominal metric tire geometry using exact conversion constants (1 in = 25.4 mm, 1 mile = 1,609,344 mm, 1 km = 1,000,000 mm).

Sidewall Height (mm)
sidewall_mm = width_mm × aspect_ratio / 100
Overall Diameter (mm)
diameter_mm = (rim_in × 25.4) + (2 × sidewall_mm)
Circumference (mm)
circumference_mm = π × diameter_mm
Revolutions per Mile & km
revs_mi = 1,609,344 / circumference_mm
revs_km = 1,000,000 / circumference_mm
Axle Clearance Change (mm)
axle_clearance_mm = (new_dia − old_dia) / 2
Speedometer & Effective Gearing
speed_ratio = new_dia / old_dia
actual_speed = indicated × speed_ratio
effective_ratio = axle_ratio × (old_dia / new_dia)
Wheel Offset Geometry (W in mm)
inner_edge_mm = (W / 2) + ET
outer_edge_mm = (W / 2) − ET

Rounding rule: Chained calculations use full double-precision floating-point values internally and round only at display time (to 0.01–0.1 units or nearest whole revolution where noted).

Limitations of Calculation

Mathematical tire calculators operate on unloaded, nominal cylinders. This tool cannot determine:

  • Physical fender, bumper, pinch-weld, or suspension clearance on your specific vehicle.
  • Brake caliper barrel or spoke clearance (wheel X-factor).
  • Static loaded radius (tire squat under vehicle weight) or tread wear depth differences.
  • Manufacturer-specific molding variations between brands or tread categories.
  • Correct tire inflation pressure or vehicle stability control / ABS calibration limits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much taller is a 285/75R17 than a 265/70R17?

Mathematically, a 285/75R17 is 56.5 mm (2.22 inches) taller in overall diameter than a 265/70R17—an increase from 802.8 mm (31.61 in) to 859.3 mm (33.83 in), or +7.04%. Because half of that diameter increase sits below the wheel hub, theoretical ground clearance at the axle increases by 28.25 mm (about 1.11 inches).

When my speedometer reads 60 mph on larger tires, am I going faster or slower?

With taller tires, your actual road speed is higher than what your speedometer indicates because the tire rolls farther with every axle revolution. Moving from 265/70R17 to 285/75R17 (+7.04% circumference) means an indicated 60 mph corresponds to approximately 64.2 mph actual speed.

Does a lower wheel offset push the wheel inward or outward?

Reducing positive wheel offset (or moving into negative offset) shifts the entire wheel further outward toward the fender and street. For example, keeping width constant and changing from ET+45 to ET+20 pushes the wheel’s centerline and outer lip 25 mm (0.98 in) farther outward.

Why doesn’t this calculator tell me if a tire will rub on my vehicle?

Sidewall and wheel codes define nominal geometry relative to the hub mounting face, not the shape of your vehicle’s wheel well. Physical rubbing depends on suspension articulation, steering lock angle, caster alignment, upper control arm position, body mounts, and actual molded tire dimensions, which vary by manufacturer.

What do the two numbers in a load index like 116/113S mean?

In a service description like 116/113S, the first number (116 = 1,250 kg or 2,756 lbs) is the maximum load rating per tire when mounted in a single-wheel setup, and the second number (113 = 1,150 kg or 2,535 lbs) is the per-tire rating when mounted on a dual-rear-wheel (dually) axle. The letter S indicates a speed rating of up to 112 mph (180 km/h).
